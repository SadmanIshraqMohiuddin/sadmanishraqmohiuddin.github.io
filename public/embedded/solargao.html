The Problem

Rural Bangladesh ran its agriculture almost entirely on diesel

Bangladesh is primarily an agrarian country - most Bangladeshis depend on agriculture for their livelihood. Irrigation is critical for food security and sustained agricultural productivity. But the vast majority of irrigation pumps were powered by diesel generators - expensive, polluting, and entirely dependent on fuel supply chains that often failed in remote rural areas.

Beyond irrigation, large swathes of rural Bangladesh had no grid connectivity at all. Hundreds of thousands of households, schools, mosques, and small businesses had no reliable access to electricity. The clean energy transition in these communities required purpose-built infrastructure - solar irrigation, mini-grids, and hybrid stations - deployed at scale across difficult terrain.

My Role

Leading field operations across a national deployment programme

As Engineering Team Lead, the primary responsibility was leading the multidisciplinary engineering and field operations team of approximately 200 personnel supporting active deployment and expansion of solar infrastructure across Bangladesh.

Beyond field leadership, the role involved geospatial analysis using ArcGIS to identify optimal installation sites from satellite datasets, project scoping and engineering documentation for new initiatives, and coordination with government agencies and international development institutions including the Asian Development Bank (ADB).

👥 Field Team Leadership

Led a ~200-person multidisciplinary engineering and field operations team across active solar deployment sites in Dinajpur, Rangpur, Thakurgaon, and Sirajganj.

Solar Hybrid Station
Solar-diesel hybrid for partial grid offset

A 130 KWP Solar-Diesel Hybrid Station was built to provide electricity to over 600 households, 96 shops, 11 schools and religious institutions, 3 rice husking mills, and 2 workshops. Modular Cold Storage
Preventing post-harvest losses for rural farmers

ADB Collaboration

Connecting rural farmers to international development finance

One of the most impactful dimensions of the Solargao programme was its collaboration with the Asian Development Bank (ADB) to enable access to clean energy for the rural farming communities who needed it most but could least afford it.

Converting an irrigation pump from diesel to solar requires upfront capital investment. For subsistence farmers in rural Bangladesh, this was often an insurmountable barrier - even when the long-term savings on fuel costs were obvious.

The ADB-backed initiative addressed this directly through agent banking and mobile financial services - providing microloans to rural farmers specifically to finance solar-powered irrigation systems. This wasn't just an energy programme - it was a financial inclusion initiative that connected Bangladesh's rural agricultural communities to formal credit systems for the first time.

Supporting the rollout of this initiative meant coordinating across engineering, financial services, government, and community stakeholders simultaneously - a cross-functional coordination challenge that directly mirrors the multi-stakeholder complexity of senior BA delivery.

The Bridge

What field operations at this scale teaches

Leading a 200-person team across remote deployment sites, coordinating with government agencies and international institutions, and applying geospatial analysis to infrastructure planning - these aren't abstract experiences. They translate directly into how complex, multi-stakeholder BA programmes are navigated today.

What this role built
Large-scale team leadership under ambiguity
Leading ~200 field personnel across remote sites with limited infrastructure required clear communication, structured coordination, and the ability to make decisions without perfect information.
Geospatial and data-driven planning
Using ArcGIS satellite analysis to identify optimal installation locations combined analytical rigour with operational planning - the same data-first approach applied to BA requirements analysis today.
Multi-stakeholder coordination
Simultaneously coordinating engineers, field teams, government agencies, local communities, and an international development bank required the same stakeholder management discipline as enterprise BA delivery.
How it shapes BA practice
Comfort with complexity and scale
Enterprise BA programmes involving 30,000+ users, government clients, and multi-workstream delivery feel familiar - the coordination complexity is structurally similar to what was navigated in the field.
Data-driven requirements thinking
Applying geospatial analysis before deploying infrastructure reinforces a core BA habit - gathering and analysing real data to inform decisions rather than relying on assumptions or intuition.
Credibility across government and institutional contexts
Working alongside ADB and government agencies calibrates the communication and documentation standards required when engaging government stakeholders - a recurring context in BA work today.
